Creative Commons is a nonprofit organisation founded in 2001 by [Lawrence Lessig](people:lawrence-lessig), Hal Abelson, and others to publish a suite of standardised, machine-readable copyright licenses that authors can apply to their work to grant some rights to the public by default. Where traditional copyright defaults to "all rights reserved," a Creative Commons license declares "some rights reserved" — granting copying, modification, commercial use, or other specific freedoms in advance, without each user having to negotiate them. The license suite (CC BY, CC BY-SA, CC BY-NC, CC0, and others) covers most of the choices an author actually wants to make.

The contribution is partly legal and partly social. The legal piece is rigorous: each license is written in a layered form — a plain-language summary, a full legal text, and machine-readable metadata — so it can be understood, enforced, and indexed at scale. The social piece is the establishment of a vocabulary that makes sharing the default and proprietary use the exception in domains where authors actually want broad reuse. Wikipedia, OpenStreetMap, much of academic open access, Flickr's public archive, and a sizable portion of the [[digital-commons|digital commons]] are licensed under CC terms.

Creative Commons fits inside the broader free-culture lineage that also produced the [[foss|GNU GPL]] and Berne-style copyleft. It extends the [[open-source]] argument from software to text, images, music, video, and data, and it gives authors a way to opt out of the worst of copyright maximalism without abandoning attribution or share-alike protections. For the parallel society it functions as legal infrastructure: it makes the [[digital-commons|commons]] enforceable, gives [[shadow-library|archival projects]] a legitimate path for redistributing covered material, and provides the standard interface between volunteer production and the wider information economy.
