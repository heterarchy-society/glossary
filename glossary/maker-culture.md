Maker culture is the contemporary continuation of the DIY tradition, organised around the belief that making things — electronics, machines, software, garments, food — is a basic form of agency rather than a hobbyist's leisure. It emphasises hands-on competence, shared tools, peer learning, and the public exchange of designs, blueprints, and recipes, with deliberate refusal of the boundary between consumer and producer.

The movement crystallised in the mid-2000s around Dale Dougherty's *Make* magazine (2005) and the first Maker Faire (2006), but its roots run further: post-war ham radio, the 1970s personal-computer hobbyists, [[hackerspace|hackerspaces]] and FabLabs, and the punk and zine subcultures all fed into it. Cheap microcontrollers (Arduino), accessible CAD tools, small-batch fabrication services, and [[open-hardware|open-hardware]] designs lowered the cost of making physical objects to a price comparable with what software previously enjoyed.

For the parallel society, maker culture is the human substrate that makes [[distributed-manufacturing|distributed manufacturing]] and open-hardware more than a hypothesis — it produces the people who can actually maintain alternative infrastructure: repair the mesh radio, debug the solar inverter, fork the open insulin pump. It also illustrates a recurring tension: the same energy that builds parallel structures also feeds a sizable consumer market for craft kits and lifestyle hobbies, which can absorb the political content of the movement and leave the structural critique behind.
