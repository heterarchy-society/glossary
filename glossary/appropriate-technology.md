Appropriate technology is the family of design approaches that select tools and techniques to match the human, ecological, and economic context in which they will be used — rather than imposing capital-intensive industrial solutions on every problem. The term and the underlying argument are most associated with E. F. Schumacher, whose *Small Is Beautiful* (1973) made the case that "gigantism" in technology was a category error, not a sign of progress. Schumacher proposed "intermediate technology" — tools more productive than handcraft but smaller, cheaper, and more decentralised than mass industry — as the practical alternative.

The appropriate-technology tradition runs in close parallel to Ivan Illich's [[conviviality|convivial tools]] and the [[vernacular]] forms of building, healing, and education that he defended. Both reject the premise that the right tool is always the largest, most centralised, and most credentialed. Both insist that the question "appropriate to what end, and to whom?" must come before the engineering specification. And both treat the maintainability of a tool by its users as a first-class design criterion rather than an afterthought.

The contemporary inheritors are scattered but recognisable. [[Open-hardware|Open hardware]] projects, [[solarpunk]] design fiction, [[distributed-manufacturing|distributed manufacturing]], the [[off-grid-living|off-grid]] movement, permaculture, and many small-scale renewable-energy efforts all carry forward the same instinct: that the right scale of a technology is set by the community that will use it, and that the work of building human-scale alternatives is more useful than the work of critiquing industrial gigantism. For the parallel society, appropriate technology is the material-culture wing of the project — the answer to "what does the parallel polis actually make and run on?"
