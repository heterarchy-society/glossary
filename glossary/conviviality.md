Conviviality is Ivan Illich's name for the quality of tools and institutions that enhance individual capability rather than replace it. A convivial tool is one that ordinary people can understand, use, and repair without specialists; one that does not impose a particular outcome on its user; one that remains effective without growing past a certain scale. A bicycle is convivial; a highway system is not. A library is convivial; a curriculum is not. The concept was developed in Tools for Conviviality (1973) as the positive counterpart to [[counterproductivity]] — the question of what to build, not just what to avoid.

[Illich](people:ivan-illich) was reacting against two then-dominant positions: the technocratic faith that more industrial production would solve human problems, and the romantic rejection of technology altogether. His third path argued that the question is not whether to use tools but which tools, evaluated by whether they expand or contract the autonomy of their users. Convivial tools have three properties: they are accessible without certification, so anyone can pick them up; they are responsive to the user's intent rather than dictating it; and they remain compatible with other tools rather than demanding monopoly. Industrial tools tend to violate all three — they require specialists, they impose their own logic on the user, and they make alternatives unviable. The mass car and the mass school are Illich's recurring examples, both having crossed from convivial to manipulative as they scaled.

The [[cypherpunk]] and free-software traditions developed in parallel and arrived at strikingly similar criteria. [[Open source]] software is convivial in Illich's sense: the source is readable, the user can modify it, alternatives can fork without permission. [[Peer-to-peer]] networks distribute the function rather than concentrating it. The question of convivial design is one of the few clear bridges between Illich's analog 1970s and contemporary digital-rights work. Not every decentralised technology is convivial, though — [[Bitcoin]] mining concentrated into industrial operations; many open-source projects became unmaintainable without paid specialists; consumer crypto wallets often hide their workings as aggressively as proprietary apps. The heterarchy question is not whether a tool calls itself decentralised, but whether the people using it can actually understand, modify, and route around it. That is the convivial test.
