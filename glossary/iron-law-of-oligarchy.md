The Iron Law of Oligarchy is the claim that every organisation tends toward control by a small group as it grows, regardless of the values it was founded on. It was formulated by sociologist Robert Michels in 1911 after studying European socialist parties and trade unions — organisations explicitly committed to worker self-rule that nonetheless produced self-perpetuating leadership classes. The democratic intentions were real. The drift happened anyway. Michels' uncomfortable conclusion was that the drift is a property of organisation itself at scale, not a failure of those particular movements.

The dynamics interlock. Coordination at scale requires specialists; specialists accumulate knowledge and relationships that ordinary members do not have time to acquire; that asymmetry becomes power; and power, once held, is defended. Leaders control which information reaches members, which shapes what members can even ask for. Members, busy with their own lives, rationally delegate — which is precisely what allows the leadership class to consolidate. Over time the organisation's continued existence becomes the de facto goal of those running it, displacing the original purpose. Michels saw no exit from the loop within the form of the mass organisation itself: revolutions that toppled one oligarchy installed another. The iron law is not corruption — it is what coordination at scale rewards. The same shape appears in parties, unions, NGOs, corporations, religious orders, and online communities once they pass a certain size.

The implication is that good values are not enough. A movement organised along conventional lines will produce a leadership class regardless of what its founding documents say. This is why heterarchical thinking treats structure as the live question: [[decentralization]] removes the central position from which oligarchisation can begin; [[voluntary association]] keeps exit available, so the leadership cannot rely on captive members; [[consensus mechanism|consensus mechanisms]] distribute the function that would otherwise concentrate in a few hands. The iron law is the organisational form of the broader pattern of [[counterproductivity]]: a tool — in this case the organisation itself — that past a certain scale begins working against what it was meant to do. Naming the pattern is the first defense; building against it is the harder work, and not all the early answers have aged well.
