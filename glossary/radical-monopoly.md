A radical monopoly exists when a single institutional form crowds out all other ways of meeting a need — not just other producers of the same kind, but the possibility of meeting the need outside that kind altogether. Cars do not just compete with bicycles; they remake cities so walking and cycling become impractical. Schools do not just outcompete other educators; they make non-credentialed knowledge invisible. [Ivan Illich](people:ivan-illich) named the concept in Tools for Conviviality (1973).

The distinction matters because antitrust thinking only sees the first kind. A market with several car manufacturers is competitive by economic measures, yet collectively those manufacturers hold a radical monopoly over mobility — alternatives are infeasible because the infrastructure has been reshaped around the product. Hospitals do not just compete with other clinics; they redefine illness so only medical intervention counts as healing. Schools redefine learning so only certified curricula count as education. The radical monopoly is rarely created by intent; it accumulates from a thousand individually rational choices to standardise around the dominant form. Once it is in place, opting out is no longer a matter of personal preference — the alternatives have been built out of the world.

For the parallel society, radical monopoly is the harder problem than concentrated ownership. A handful of platforms hold radical monopoly over digital social life: opting out is technically possible but socially costly because network effects, search rankings, and default communication habits have all been built around them. [[Decentralization]] of ownership does not by itself solve this; a decentralised network nobody can find is not a real alternative. The work is building parallel infrastructure good enough to actually use — protocols, communities, and tools someone can switch to without losing what made the original useful. [[Counter-economics]] and [[agorism]] are economic strategies in this direction; [[peer-to-peer]] and [[open source]] tools, technical ones. The honest measurement is not whether an alternative exists but whether ordinary people can live in it.
