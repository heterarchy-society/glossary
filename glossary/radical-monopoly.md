A radical monopoly exists when a single institutional form crowds out all other ways of meeting a need — not just other producers of the same kind, but the possibility of meeting the need outside that kind altogether. Cars do not just compete with horses and bicycles; they remake cities so that walking and cycling become impractical. Schools do not just outcompete other educators; they make non-credentialed knowledge socially invisible. The concept comes from Ivan Illich's Tools for Conviviality (1973) and names a more total form of capture than economic monopoly.

The distinction matters because antitrust thinking only sees the first kind. A market with several car manufacturers is competitive by economic measures, yet collectively those manufacturers can hold a radical monopoly over mobility — they make alternatives infeasible by reshaping the infrastructure around their product. The same pattern recurs across domains. Hospitals do not just compete with other clinics; they redefine illness so that only medical intervention counts as healing. Schools do not just compete with other schools; they redefine learning so that only certified curricula count as education. Mass transit replaces a city designed for legs. The radical monopoly is rarely created by intent; it accumulates from a thousand individually rational choices to standardise around the dominant form. Once it is in place, opting out is no longer a matter of personal preference — the alternatives have been built out of the world.

For the parallel society, radical monopoly is the harder problem than concentrated ownership. A handful of platforms hold radical monopoly over digital social life: opting out is technically possible but socially costly, because the network effects, the search ranking, and the default communication habits have all been built around them. [[Decentralization]] of ownership does not by itself solve this; a decentralised network that nobody can find is not a real alternative. The work is building parallel infrastructure that is good enough to actually use — protocols, communities, and tools someone can switch to without losing what made the original useful. [[Counter-economics]] and [[agorism]] are economic strategies in this direction; [[peer-to-peer]] and [[open source]] tools are technical ones. The honest measurement is not whether an alternative exists but whether ordinary people can live in it.
