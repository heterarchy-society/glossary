Vernacular, in [Ivan Illich](people:ivan-illich)'s later writing, names everything people do for themselves outside both market and state — subsistence, mutual aid, gift, the daily practices nobody pays for and nobody administers. From Latin vernaculus, "belonging to the household." Illich used it to recover a category industrial society has made nearly invisible: the uncommodified activity that sustains both market and state from beneath.

Illich developed the idea across Shadow Work (1981) and Gender (1982). Industrial society, he argued, depends on a hidden substrate of unpaid work — cooking, caring, commuting, organising — without which the priced economy would collapse. The vernacular is what gets enclosed when an institution colonises a domain. A neighbourhood that watched its own children becomes a childcare market plus a regulatory regime. A village that healed itself becomes a clinic's clientele. Each enclosure replaces non-monetised reciprocity with priced services.

Heterarchical thinking takes the vernacular seriously as the substrate any parallel society grows from. [[Mutualism]], [[the-commons|commons]], gift economies, and informal apprenticeships are functional alternatives to wage labour and state provision. Digital practice extends the vernacular: [[open source]] is a vernacular form of software production; [[peer-to-peer]] sharing, of distribution. None is automatically safe from enclosure — free software gets absorbed into corporate stacks, decentralised protocols route through a handful of providers. It is the same project the [[parallel society]] has always been, in older language.
