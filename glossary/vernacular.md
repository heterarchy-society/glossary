Vernacular, in Ivan Illich's later writing, names everything people do for themselves outside both market and state — subsistence, mutual aid, gift, shared knowledge, the practices of everyday life that nobody pays for and nobody administers. The word comes from the Latin vernaculus, "belonging to the household": the local, the unofficial, the homemade. Illich used it to recover a category that industrial society has made nearly invisible — the immense amount of human activity that has never been commodified or regulated, and which sustains both market and state from beneath.

[Illich](people:ivan-illich) developed the concept across Shadow Work (1981) and Gender (1982) as a corrective to the assumption that all economic activity could be measured in monetary terms. Industrial society, he argued, depends on a hidden substrate of unpaid work — cooking, caring, commuting, conversing, organising — without which the priced economy would collapse. The vernacular domain operates on different logic from either market exchange or bureaucratic allocation: it relies on direct relationship, mutual obligation, and tacit knowledge that resists formalisation. The vernacular is what gets enclosed when an institution colonises a domain. A neighbourhood that watched its own children becomes a market for childcare plus a regulatory regime around it. A village that healed itself becomes a clientele for a clinic. Each enclosure transfers competence from the household to specialists and replaces non-monetised reciprocity with priced services. The losses are largely invisible because the new arrangement is what gets measured.

Heterarchical thinking takes the vernacular seriously as the substrate that any parallel society actually has to grow from. [[Mutualism]], [[the-commons|commons]], gift economies, and informal apprenticeships are not nostalgic curiosities but functional alternatives to wage labour and state provision. Digital practice extends the vernacular into new domains: [[open source]] is a vernacular form of software production; [[peer-to-peer]] sharing is a vernacular form of distribution; community-run forums and shared infrastructure are vernacular forms of governance. None of these is automatically resistant to enclosure — many free software projects have been absorbed into corporate stacks, and many decentralised protocols end up routed through a handful of providers. The work is to keep vernacular practice viable not as a hobby but as something a person could actually live within. That is the same project the [[parallel society]] has always been, in older language.
