Vernacular, in Ivan Illich's later writing, names everything people do for themselves outside both market and state — subsistence, mutual aid, gift, shared knowledge, the daily practices nobody pays for and nobody administers. The word comes from the Latin vernaculus, "belonging to the household." Illich used it to recover a category industrial society has made nearly invisible: the immense, uncommodified activity that sustains both market and state from beneath.

[Illich](people:ivan-illich) developed the concept across Shadow Work (1981) and Gender (1982) as a corrective to the assumption that all economic activity could be measured in money. Industrial society, he argued, depends on a hidden substrate of unpaid work — cooking, caring, commuting, organising — without which the priced economy would collapse. The vernacular runs on direct relationship, mutual obligation, and tacit knowledge that resists formalisation. It is what gets enclosed when an institution colonises a domain. A neighbourhood that watched its own children becomes a childcare market plus a regulatory regime around it. A village that healed itself becomes a clinic's clientele. Each enclosure transfers competence to specialists and replaces non-monetised reciprocity with priced services — losses largely invisible because the new arrangement is what gets measured.

Heterarchical thinking takes the vernacular seriously as the substrate any parallel society actually grows from. [[Mutualism]], [[the-commons|commons]], gift economies, and informal apprenticeships are not nostalgic curiosities but functional alternatives to wage labour and state provision. Digital practice extends the vernacular into new domains: [[open source]] is a vernacular form of software production; [[peer-to-peer]] sharing, a vernacular form of distribution; community-run forums, a vernacular form of governance. None of this is automatically safe from enclosure — many free software projects get absorbed into corporate stacks, and many decentralised protocols end up routed through a handful of providers. The work is to keep vernacular practice viable not as a hobby but as something a person could live within. That is the same project the [[parallel society]] has always been, in older language.
