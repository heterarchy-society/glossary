WikiLeaks is a publishing organization, founded in 2006 by [Julian Assange](people:julian-assange) and a small group of activists and technologists, that specializes in releasing classified, censored, or otherwise restricted documents of public interest. Its model combines journalism with cypherpunk infrastructure: an [[anonymity|anonymous]] submission system originally based on [[tor|Tor]] and PGP, a global mirror network resistant to takedown, and a deliberate practice of publishing source documents in full alongside any narrative reporting. Major releases include the Collateral Murder video (2010), the Iraq and Afghan War Logs, Cablegate (the U.S. diplomatic cables), and the Vault 7 disclosures of CIA hacking tools.

WikiLeaks is the most consequential operational expression of [[cryptoanarchy|cryptoanarchist]] ideas to date. It demonstrated that strong [[encryption]], distributed hosting, and a small group of disciplined operators could publish material that the most powerful intelligence agencies in the world could not prevent or recall. The response — financial blockades by payment processors, prolonged legal pressure on Assange, the prosecution of [[whistleblowing|whistleblower]] sources — confirmed the cypherpunk hypothesis that strategic privacy and infrastructure independence were preconditions for this kind of work to continue at all.

The legacy is contested. WikiLeaks reshaped the practice of journalism, gave rise to a generation of secure-submission tools used by mainstream outlets (SecureDrop, GlobaLeaks), and made [[sousveillance]] of state institutions a technically tractable project. It also became a cautionary case study in the personal costs of running such an operation and the difficulty of separating publishing infrastructure from the political reputation of any single individual. Either way, WikiLeaks established a template — encrypted submission, distributed hosting, full-text publication — that has become standard equipment for press freedom and [[transparency]] organizations worldwide.
